## FRAME 2: THE TOPONYMY DISCOVERY
**Title on board:** *"The Ville de Montréal Gives an Answer"*

**Content:**
- You searched the Ville de Montréal Toponymie database
- Result: "Rue de la Police" was officially designated **April 10, 1876**
- The street was named **because of a combined police and fire station** built the previous year — 1875
- That station was **Caserne 10**, designed by architect **Henri-Maurice Perrault**
- Original address: **1625 rue Sainte-Catherine Ouest** (later renumbered to 1234)
- The 19th-century practice: police + fire under one roof → the street took its name from the building

## FRAME 3: THE FIRST CASERNE 10 (1875–1931)
**Title on board:** *"The Original Station: Perrault's Combined Police & Fire Hall"*

**Content:**
- Built 1875 by Henri-Maurice Perrault
- Combined police station and fire station — a common civic model of the era
- Context: Montreal Fire Department established 1863 under Chief Alexander Bertram — just 12 years before
- Transition from volunteer to professional firefighting
- The station served during the horse-drawn era — hand pumps, then steam engines
- The Goad fire insurance atlas (1890) shows it amid a mixed residential/light industrial neighbourhood (S.H. & J. Moss textile warehouse nearby)
- By the 1912 Goad atlas, the corridor along Ste-Catherine had intensified commercially

## FRAME 4: THE SECOND CASERNE 10 (1931–PRESENT)
**Title on board:** *"Art Deco on Saint-Mathieu: The Station That Replaced It"*

**Content:**
- New building at **1445 rue Saint-Mathieu** (around the corner, half a block away)
- Designed by **Harold E. Shorey & S. Douglass Ritchie**, 1931
- Art Deco / "Style Camillien Houde" — part of Mayor Houde's Depression-era public works program
- Won the **1931 RAIC award** for architectural design
- Purpose-built for **motorized apparatus** — no more horse stalls
- Part of a wave: Casernes 23, 31, 41, 48 all built 1931–32 in similar style
- **Still in active service today** — one of Montreal's oldest continuously operating stations
- The memorial plaque inside honours firefighters who died in the line of duty from both buildings

## FRAME 6: THE GREY NUNS FIRE — FEBRUARY 14, 1918
**Title on board:** *"Valentine's Day, 1918: The Fire That Tested Caserne 10"*

**This is the critical link.** The Grey Nuns Motherhouse was on Guy Street at Dorchester — *one to two blocks from Caserne 10's 1875 location on Ste-Catherine*. When alarm box No. 419 sounded at 7:50 PM, Caserne 10 was almost certainly among the very first stations to respond.

### Sub-section A: What Happened
- Evening of Feb. 14, 1918 — fire breaks out on the top floor (5th/6th) of the west wing, St-Mathieu Street side
- **170+ babies** aged a few days to 2.5 years asleep in the crèche dormitory
- Fire spread rapidly through wooden interior — curtains caught first
- 285 wounded WWI soldiers were hospitalized on floors below
- Private alarm No. 419 at 7:50 PM → 2nd alarm immediately → 3rd alarm at 8:07 PM
- **18 fire stations** responded to the general alarm
- **53–60+ infants killed** — many bodies unrecognizable

### Sub-section B: The Fire Department Response
- **Sub-Chief (sous-chef) Marin** — "chef de district" — first fire official on scene
- He and his men rescued dozens of children, carrying four at a time
- **Chief Tremblay** — named in Le Devoir as leading firefighters to reach the dormitory
- **Dr. Lafleur** and **Dr. Lamoureux** — brigade physicians, treated injured firefighters on site
- Water pressure was weak — had to set up steam pumps and run hoses a considerable distance
- Firefighters, soldiers, and nuns worked together until Marin ordered them to stop — the dormitory had become a furnace

### Sub-section C: The Caserne 10 Connection
- Caserne 10 at 1625 Ste-Catherine was **the closest fire station** to the Grey Nuns Motherhouse
- Sub-Chief Marin as "district chief" likely commanded the district that included the Caserne 10 territory
- The 1918 fire happened 13 years before the station was rebuilt — the firefighters who responded came from the **original 1875 Perrault building**
- This was the most catastrophic fire in the station's service area during its existence

### Sub-section D: The Arson Revelation
- Initial cause reported in all 1918 newspapers: electrical short circuit near curtains
- **Later investigation revealed arson**: **Berthe Courtemanche**, a nursery worker hired only 6 weeks before, set the fire
- Tried in late 1918, deemed unfit to stand trial
- Transferred to Hôpital Saint-Jean-de-Dieu (psychiatric asylum)
- Died there ~10 years later of tuberculosis

## FRAME 7: BROADER THEMES — WHY THIS MATTERS
**Title on board:** *"What One Fire Station Tells Us About a City"*

This frame pulls the threads together into the analytical argument. Possible thematic zones:

### Theme 1: Policing, Fires, and Infrastructure
- The combined police/fire model of the 1870s → the street name as a fossil of civic organization
- Professionalization of firefighting: 1863 paid department → alarm telegraph systems → motorized apparatus
- How infrastructure investment reflects political priorities (Houde's Depression-era public works)

### Theme 2: Institutional Adjacency — The Grey Nuns Block
- The station and the Motherhouse were neighbours for 43 years (1875–1918)
- The fire exposed the limits of the 19th-century infrastructure: weak water pressure, no motorized equipment yet, wooden interiors in a massive stone building
- The same neighbourhood that housed orphans, wounded soldiers, nuns, and firefighters

### Theme 3: Memory and Forgetting
- The street name "Rue de la Police" is the only surviving trace of the original 1875 building
- The memorial plaque inside Caserne 10 honours firefighters who died — but no public marker connects the station to the Grey Nuns fire
- The 1918 fire killed 53–60+ infants and is largely forgotten outside Concordia ghost stories
- The arson finding (Courtemanche) adds a layer of deliberate violence that the initial "accident" narrative obscured

### Theme 4: Continuity of Service
- Same location, two buildings, 150 years of continuous fire protection
- The 1931 rebuild wasn't just architectural modernization — it was a response to the evolving risks the station had witnessed firsthand

**CONNECTS TO →** All previous frames (this is the analytical hub)

## KEY ARGUMENT TO THREAD THROUGH
*A street name that nobody notices — "Rue de la Police" — is the last surviving trace of a 19th-century civic building that shaped its neighbourhood. Following that trace reveals not just one fire station's architectural history, but a catastrophe that killed 60 infants one block away, a forgotten arsonist, and the men who ran from this station into the flames. The street name is a fossil. The research is an excavation.*
