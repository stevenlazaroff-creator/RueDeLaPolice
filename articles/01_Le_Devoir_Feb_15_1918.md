# More Than 50 Babies Perish in the Flames

**Source:** *Le Devoir*, Montreal, Friday, February 15, 1918, Vol. IX, No. 39

---

## A Violent Fire Breaks Out Last Night in the West Wing of the Grey Nuns' General Hospital, on Saint-Mathieu Street, and a Large Number of Very Young Children Cannot Be Saved — The Nuns Display Heroic Devotion — More Than 1,100 People Were Inside the Burning Building — The Causes of the Fire

Like a trail of gunpowder, the news spread last night: "Fire at the Grey Nuns!" and for an hour it was a procession of fire engines, ambulances, each with their siren cries spreading alarm throughout the city. The curious hurried toward the scene of the disaster, and soon a large crowd was massed on the outskirts of the building in flames.

When the firefighters arrived on the scene, the entire west wing was ablaze, and the Sisters were already saving the babies who were in that part of the building, with a devotion worthy of all praise. The black cornettes moved without concern for the flames through the long, dark corridors and descended carrying in their arms one, two, or three babies, then returned to save still more of these little beings, until the flames had completely blocked their path. Meanwhile, amid the crackling of the flames destroying everything, one could hear the cries of the little victims dying in the flames without anyone being able to help them. There were then scenes of desolation.

Around 10:30, forty small charred bodies were recovered; it is estimated that the number of victims is more than fifty, all children from a few days to two and a half years old.

During this time, the soldiers hospitalized in the institution, the firefighters, and charitable people helped transport the elderly, the infirm, and the sick. Without panic and with remarkable order, the rescue was organized: the sick were brought down on stretchers and elderly people who had not been outside for several years were transported into the street in their wheelchairs, supported by soldiers while the ambulances, patrol wagons, and private automobiles transported all these people to Mont Sainte-Marie on Guy Street, to hospitals, to neighboring houses, and to the Montefiore Club.

### A Third Alarm

When the third alarm was sounded, around 8 o'clock, 18 fire stations responded to the alarm and began flooding the building which was still burning.

And scenes of devotion were witnessed: soldiers, without concern for the danger, passed through the flames, reached the dormitory, and returned with several children in their arms. One soldier in particular, whose name is unknown, made four such trips and succeeded in saving several babies. But suddenly, the flames increased in intensity, spread into the stairwell, burst from the windows, and the rescue had to be abandoned.

The number of casualties was not yet known last night, but it will surely exceed the number of 50; none of the hospital patients perished, as far as can be known this morning, but several had to be transported to other hospitals, suffering from nervous shock. The ambulances transported 25 to Notre-Dame Hospital, 5 to the General Hospital, one to the Royal Victoria, and one to the Western Hospital.

### Mgr. the Archbishop and the Mayor on the Scene

Mgr. Bruchési and Mayor Martin were at the scene of the fire as soon as they learned the news.

### Heroism of the Nuns

The electricity failed a few moments after the fire broke out, and the rescue was all the more difficult because one had to find a way through corridors and dark stairways, filled with thick smoke. The firefighters, led by Chief Tremblay, then resolved to reach the dormitory, where one could still hear the cries of the victims, by means of the fire escapes. A few were saved this way, but soon this approach also had to be abandoned because the flames had caught the bedding and mattresses, and acrid smoke was pouring from every opening. It was then that two or three nuns, who despite everything persisted in trying to enter the building, lost consciousness and were treated by Doctors Lafleur and Lamoureux, physicians of the brigade.

From the start of the fire, the members of the Montefiore Club placed their building at the disposal of the nuns. Several helpless people and a large number of babies were transported there. The babies were laid on the billiard tables, while women improvised themselves as mothers and consoled the poor little ones awakened too suddenly. Dr. Leduc took care of those who were ill, and milk was sent for. After a few minutes, several babies continued their sleep as if nothing had happened.

Around 10:30, the firefighters discovered in the ruins thirty-eight small charred bodies; most had their faces turned toward the ground, as if by instinct of self-preservation, the children had tried to protect themselves with their pillows against the flames that were burning them.

The firefighters had to work hard, for it was necessary to prevent the destructive element from spreading to the north wing, and the fire was spreading through the basement. They had to dig holes in the walls and floors to direct streams of water, and after some time of arduous struggle, the danger had passed on that side. They completely flooded all the lower floors of the other wings, which at every moment threatened to catch fire.

Around 11 o'clock, the flames finally diminished in intensity and some nuns were seen returning, entering their house to resume work courageously, despite the misfortune that had just befallen them. Makeshift shelters for the night were organized in the parts of the institution that had been spared. Friends and soldiers set to work and order was restored, while the firefighters finished extinguishing the flames.

Lieutenant-Colonel F. Smith, in charge of the military hospitals of Montreal, declared last night that not a single soldier had perished. All are summoned this morning to No. 68 Drummond Street.

### An Accident to the Mayor

Mayor Martin was struck and knocked down by a carriage and was injured. During the evening, he went to Notre-Dame Hospital, where the doctors treated him. It is believed the mayor broke a rib, but no bones were fractured.

### History of the Hospital

The Grey Nuns' hospital was founded in 1755, near the river. In 1871, they moved to the current location, at the corner of Guy and Dorchester streets. It is the mother house of the Grey Nuns, who have several houses throughout the country.

The institution that was destroyed last night serves to hospitalize the sick and the elderly; one of them was 98 years old at the time of the fire. The Sisters also had the Crèche, which was destroyed yesterday. There they kept, fed, and raised foundlings or abandoned babies.

Since the beginning of the war, part of the institution served to hospitalize invalid soldiers returning from the front.

### Interview with Reverend Sister Marie du Sacré-Cœur

This morning, a representative of *Le Devoir* was able to speak with Reverend Sister Marie du Sacré-Cœur, who had been in charge of the Crèche. The account she gives us of the scenes that unfolded during last night's fire is moving and heartbreaking. The unfortunate nun is devastated by the death of her dear little ones, but let us listen:

"The room where the fire started is located in the center of our house, on the top floor. It was inhabited by twenty-four babies aged from a few days old up to two and a half years. Some were orphans who had lost a father or mother; most were foundlings. How the fire started in this room remains a mystery, but we believe that an electrical wire passing through large curtains, as a result of a short circuit, set the fire, which then spread to the cradles in which the unfortunate children were sleeping. From there, the flames extended to the other rooms, where there were also a large number of them.

"These babies go to bed at five in the evening and are under the guard of ten nuns who watch all night. At seven-twenty, everything was normal in the rooms. No smell of smoke could be detected. At seven-thirty, the central room was in flames.

"The firefighters were called immediately, and our nuns and soldiers organized the first aid — the room was nothing but a blazing furnace. The children were crying. The older ones were calling for 'mama.' How to rescue them? We had before us a wall of flames. Nuns forced their way through and were able to save some of these innocents.

"One nun, Sister Côté, took four under her two arms, but suffocated by the smoke and threatened by the flames, she fell to the ground, but was saved by a firefighter along with the babies. She had saved twelve previously. At one point, it was impossible to enter the center. It was also impossible to communicate with the other rooms. It was then necessary to use rescue ladders to reach the burning floor and save the children in the side rooms.

"The journey was long, and the flames continued to crackle with more frenzy than ever, because there was a lack of water. That is what explains why the flames made so much progress.

"The number of victims cannot yet be determined. It will certainly exceed 60. All our elderly men and women, the sick soldiers returning from the front, have been placed in safety, and none suffered injuries or bruises."

A very elderly woman who had been dying since yesterday morning was transported urgently to Notre-Dame Hospital in the evening. This morning, she was in the same condition.

"But, Sister, were all your boarders able to return to the Mother House after the fire?" — "Yes, all, except the children, whom we transported to Sainte-Justine, to the Miséricorde, to Notre-Dame Hospital, and to Saint-Paul Hospital, because all our cradles and small beds were destroyed by the fire."

"Can you give the exact number of victims?" — "No, because we are busy identifying them, in order to know which children are missing. The number will probably exceed 60."

"Are the losses caused by the flames considerable?" — "Yes, and I believe our insurance will barely cover them."

Asked about the probable reconstruction of this wing, Reverend Sister Marie du Sacré-Cœur told us that she did not know.

The personnel residing in these rooms at the time of the fire was as follows: 20 nuns, 20 nurses, 85 elderly men, 105 elderly women, and 285 soldiers. The number of babies was about two hundred. All those over two years old were saved.

### The Pilgrimage This Morning

Inside the hospice this morning, one encountered only the saddened faces of nuns and mothers. The latter came to reclaim the baby they had entrusted to the nuns, because they were too poor to raise them.

The ambulances were transporting babies who were crying and wailing. A nurse accompanied them. These small white bundles, well swaddled, moved many people, and several passersby who thought of their own preferred to leave rather than witness these departures of innocents whom savage death had spared.

### Bodies Taken to the Morgue

The morgue van transported 53 victims of last night's fire this morning. All the small bodies are absolutely unrecognizable; they are completely charred. The coroner will begin an inquest tomorrow morning.

### At Notre-Dame Hospital

Twenty-seven people, including three nuns — Sisters Mailloux, Hubert, and Bernard — were taken to Notre-Dame Hospital during the night. All were suffering from nervous shock. Sister Bernard was burned while saving children. She was nevertheless well enough this morning to return to the Convent on Guy Street.
