# Many Infants Perish in Fire at Montreal

**Source:** *The Montreal Weekly Witness*, Tuesday, February 19, 1918

---

## Foundlings, in Wing of Grey Nuns' Home, Could Not Be Reached in Time

More than fifty infant lives were lost in a fire which broke out on the top floor in the west wing of the great Guy Street Home of the Grey Nuns, Montreal, about eight o'clock on Thursday night.

The fire originated on the top floor of the St. Matthew Street wing, near a tower, supposedly from electric wires, and immediately caught the curtain of a nearby window, from which it spread rapidly throughout the wooden interior of the topmost storey.

All of this floor was occupied by babes in cots, some of them only a few days old, little unwanted tots left by distressed mothers on the doorstep of the convent to be cared for by the kind nuns. These were the infants lost. The older children, who were in another part of the building, were saved.

On the floors immediately below were returned soldiers who were still undergoing hospital treatment, and some awaiting discharge. That portion of the building had been given over by the nuns shortly after the outbreak of the war as a hospital under the administration of the Military Hospitals Commission command. About two hundred soldiers slept in the building, and about one hundred others received day treatment there.

### Touching Rescue Scenes

In the other portions of the building were patients ranging in years from a few days to almost a hundred years, all of whom were taken out through the Guy Street entrance. Bed-ridden men and women and aged nuns, some of whom had not seen the outside of the institution for a score of years, were carried out in cots, wheel chairs and blankets, mostly by soldiers, policemen, and voluntary workers who had hastened to the scene. Long years of pain had seared the features of many with deep lines, and snow-white hair crowned many a head. The shock of their sudden removal was expressed on many faces, but the rescuers worked with great tenderness and soon had their charges housed in nearby homes.

The Montefiore Club, on Guy Street, took in babies, nuns, and aged women patients; many nuns were taken to the Mont Sainte-Marie Academy on Guy Street just below Dorchester, and hundreds of others to nearby houses. Every hospital ambulance in the city was there, supplemented by police patrol sleighs, but as these were soon seen to be inadequate the military authorities were called on for military ambulances, trucks and transport cars. Scores of privately owned automobiles, limousines and runabouts also appeared on the scene, and all were pressed into service.

Twenty-five patients were taken at once to Notre-Dame Hospital, five to the General, one to the Royal Victoria, and one to the Western, none of whom was injured, but some suffering greatly from nervous shock.

News of the fire spread rapidly throughout the city, and attracted many thousands of spectators, all of whom wanted to assist, but seeing the danger of the crowd getting in the way, a hundred or more of the spectators themselves joined with the police and soldiers in forming a cordon about the Guy Street entrance whence the inmates were being taken out.

### Recovery of Bodies

The most pitiful sight of the night came when firemen reported, about half-past ten o'clock, that they had located the dead bodies of several children. By 11 o'clock, thirty-eight little charred and blackened bodies had been recovered, and under the direction of Police Lieutenant Paisley, of the Salvage Corps, were laid out on the floor of one of the play rooms in the north end of the west wing. Many of the remains were unrecognizable, but in the majority of cases it is believed the little ones were suffocated with smoke before the flames reached them.

At that time, the fire had been practically extinguished with the exception of the tower about the centre of the Matthew Street wing of the building, and the prevention of its further spread was assured. All the southern portion of the top storey had been destroyed, and a great deal of damage done to the remaining portion of the west wing.

### Heroic Soldiers and Nurses

Heroic work on the part of some hundreds of returned soldiers and nursing sisters in rescuing the children prevented further loss of life. The first of the firemen, Sub-Chief Marin among them, arrived quickly on the scene, but the soldiers were already at work, at great risk to themselves, in handing children down the fire escapes. Nearly the whole upper floor was then ablaze.

The firemen rushed in and seized children right and left, Sub-Chief Marin taking four in his arms at a time, but for only a very few minutes were they able to work. A sudden gust of flame and smoke which burst from the tower made it impossible to reach children still lying in their cots in that part of the building, but only upon the definite orders of the fire chief were the soldiers debarred from attempting further rescues.

Mayor Martin, who was hastening to the scene after the general alarm, was struck by a carriage while walking along the street. The Mayor was knocked down and suffered a severe bruise on his left side.
