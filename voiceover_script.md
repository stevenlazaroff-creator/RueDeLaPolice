# Voiceover Narration Script
## Caserne 10: From a Street Name to a Catastrophe

**Estimated total runtime: 12–14 minutes**

## PART 1: THE STREET
**[~1.5 minutes]**
**[VISUAL: Photo of Rue de la Police street sign / your Monday walk route]**

Every Monday this semester, I walk the same route. Out of the John Molson School of Business, south down Guy Street, towards Sainte-Catherine. It's a nothing walk. Five minutes, maybe six. You pass storefronts, you dodge traffic, you don't think about it.

But there's a moment on that walk where you cross a back lane. And unlike most back lanes in Montreal — the ones that don't even bother with a name — this one has a proper street sign. It reads: *"Rue de la Police."*

[PAUSE]

Police Street.

Now that drove me a little crazy. There is no police station anywhere near this lane. There hasn't been one in living memory. There are dozens of streets in Montreal that sit right next to actual police stations, and none of *them* are called Rue de la Police. So why does a narrow service lane, behind a row of commercial buildings on Sainte-Catherine, carry this name?

I couldn't let it go. So I looked it up.

---

## PART 2: THE ANSWER IN THE DATABASE
**[~1.5 minutes]**
**[VISUAL: Screenshot of Ville de Montréal Toponymie database entry for Rue de la Police]**

The Ville de Montréal maintains a toponymy database — an official record of how every street in the city got its name. The entry for Rue de la Police is short, but it tells you everything.

Date of designation: April 10th, 1876. And the explanation — I'll translate loosely from the French — says that in 1875, the architect Henri-Maurice Perrault built Station Number 10 — a combined police and fire station — and behind it, a narrow service lane was opened. That lane became Rue de la Police. And it kept the name even after the station was demolished in 1931.

[PAUSE]

So the street isn't named after a police station that's nearby. It's named after a police station that *used to be* nearby — a building that's been gone for almost a hundred years. The name is a fossil. A trace of something that no longer exists, embedded in the geography of the city, hiding in plain sight.

**[VISUAL: Annotated modern map showing the lane's position relative to the old station site]**

And that building — Station Number 10, *Caserne 10* — turned out to be the beginning of a much bigger story.

---

## PART 3: THE FIRST CASERNE 10
**[~2 minutes]**
**[VISUAL: Hopkins Atlas 1879, then Goad Atlas 1890]**

Caserne 10 was built in 1875 at what was then 1625 rue Sainte-Catherine Ouest. It was designed by Henri-Maurice Perrault as a combined police and fire station — which was standard practice in Montreal at the time. You put both services under one roof, you saved money, and you anchored a civic presence in the neighbourhood.

**[VISUAL: Map showing station location in context of surrounding blocks]**

This was only twelve years after the Montreal Fire Department had been formally established as a professional, paid service in 1863, under Chief Alexander Bertram. Before that, firefighting in Montreal was a volunteer operation. The professionalization was driven by a series of disasters — most notably the Great Fire of 1852, which left ten thousand people homeless and destroyed nearly half the city's housing. After that, the city got serious. Paid firefighters. Steam engines. An alarm telegraph system. And purpose-built stations like Caserne 10.

**[VISUAL: Transition from 1890 Goad Atlas to 1912 Goad Atlas — same area]**

The fire insurance atlases tell you what was happening around the station. In 1890, the Goad Atlas shows a mixed neighbourhood — residential buildings alongside light industry, including the S.H. and J. Moss textile warehouse, one of the largest garment operations in Canada. By the 1912 edition, the area along Sainte-Catherine had intensified dramatically. More commercial buildings, denser development, higher fire risk. The station was increasingly surrounded by exactly the kind of urban fabric that needed it most.

---

## PART 4: ONE BLOCK AWAY — THE GREY NUNS
**[~1 minute]**
**[VISUAL: Historical photo of Grey Nuns Motherhouse / map showing proximity to Caserne 10]**

Now, less than two blocks from Caserne 10, on Guy Street at Dorchester — what we now call René-Lévesque Boulevard — sat one of the largest institutional buildings in Montreal: the Motherhouse of the Grey Nuns.

**[VISUAL: Annotated 1912 map showing both buildings]**

By 1918, this massive stone complex housed several hundred nuns, elderly residents, a military convalescent hospital with nearly three hundred wounded soldiers from the First World War — and on the top floor of the west wing, a crèche. A nursery. Home to roughly a hundred and seventy babies and infants, most of them foundlings — abandoned children, left on the doorstep of the convent by mothers who couldn't care for them. Some were only a few days old. None were older than about two and a half.

[PAUSE]

On the evening of February 14th, 1918 — Valentine's Day — someone in the neighbourhood would have heard the alarm bells.

---

## PART 5: THE FIRE
**[~3 minutes]**
**[VISUAL: La Patrie front page, February 15, 1918]**

The fire broke out sometime between seven-thirty and seven-fifty in the evening, on the top floor of the west wing — the Saint-Mathieu Street side of the building. The babies had been put to bed hours earlier, at five o'clock, as was routine.

**[VISUAL: Diagram or annotated photo showing where the fire started]**

According to the initial reports, an electrical short circuit sent sparks into a curtain near one of the windows. The flames caught immediately and spread through the wooden interior of the top storey with terrifying speed. Smoke began filtering down through the floorboards into the military hospital wards below.

**[VISUAL: Le Canada article excerpt — alarm sequence]**

At 7:50 PM, the Grey Nuns' private alarm — Box Number 419 — sounded. Almost immediately, a second alarm called the second section of the brigade. And at 8:07, a third alarm went out, calling the reserve. According to Le Devoir, eighteen fire stations ultimately responded.

**[VISUAL: Map showing Caserne 10 location relative to the Motherhouse — highlight the proximity]**

And the first firefighters on the scene? The newspapers name Sub-Chief Marin — described as the district chief — as one of the very first to respond to that initial alarm. Caserne 10, sitting barely a block and a half away on Sainte-Catherine, was almost certainly the closest station to the fire. The men who ran out of that 1875 Perrault building and up the street towards the glow on Guy Street — they would have been among the first to arrive.

**[VISUAL: Montreal Weekly Witness article excerpt]**

What they found was chaos. The entire top floor was engulfed. Soldiers — many of them still recovering from wounds — were already evacuating themselves and carrying children down fire escapes. Nuns were rushing into smoke-filled dormitories. One nun, Sister Côté, made four separate trips into the burning floor, pulling out twelve babies on her first three trips, four more on her last.

Sub-Chief Marin and his firefighters pushed in and grabbed children from their cribs — Marin himself carrying four at a time. But they could only work for minutes. A sudden gust of flame and smoke burst from the central tower and made it impossible to go further.

[PAUSE]

Marin gave the order to stop. No more rescue attempts. The dormitory had become, as one paper described it, *une fournaise ardente* — a burning furnace.

**[VISUAL: In Memoriam images]**

By half past ten that night, firefighters had recovered thirty-eight small, charred bodies, laid out in rows on the floor of a playroom in the north wing. The final count would climb to fifty-three confirmed dead, with estimates reaching over sixty. Many of the remains were unrecognizable. Some infants may have been entirely consumed by the fire, their bodies never recovered.

[LONG PAUSE]

## PART 6: WHAT THE NEWSPAPERS TELL US
**[~1.5 minutes]**
**[VISUAL: Grid of newspaper front pages — La Patrie, Le Devoir, Le Canada, Montreal Weekly Witness]**

I was able to locate eight newspaper accounts of this fire in the archives at BAnQ — the Bibliothèque et Archives nationales du Québec. Seven in French, one in English. They range from the day after the fire to a week later, and together they build a remarkably detailed picture.

Le Devoir gave the most comprehensive account — naming Chief Tremblay, describing the water pressure problems, noting that steam pumps had to be set up at a considerable distance. Le Canada provided the precise alarm sequence and identified the brigade physicians — Doctor Lafleur and Doctor Lamoureux — who treated injured firefighters on site. La Patrie published an interview with Sister Marie du Sacré-Coeur, who described the rescue from inside the building. And three days later, La Patrie covered the coroner's inquest, where Sub-Chief Marin testified as the first witness.

**[VISUAL: List of the 24 identified victims with ages from La Patrie]**

La Patrie also published the only list I've found of identified victims — twenty-four names. Children like Blanche Raymond, six months old. Albert Auclair, fifteen months. Irène Malo, eighteen months. Most were under a year.

---

## PART 7: THE ARSON
**[~1 minute]**
**[VISUAL: Text on screen — "Berthe Courtemanche"]**

Every single newspaper in February 1918 attributed the fire to an electrical short circuit. That was the official explanation at the time of the coroner's inquest.

[PAUSE]

But later investigation told a different story. The fire was arson. It was set deliberately by a woman named Berthe Courtemanche — a nursery worker who had been hired at the Motherhouse only six weeks before the fire. She was arrested, tried, and found unfit to stand trial at the end of 1918. She was transferred to Hôpital Saint-Jean-de-Dieu — a psychiatric institution — where she died roughly ten years later.

**[VISUAL: Fade to black, then to the memorial plaque inside Caserne 10]**

None of the 1918 newspaper articles mention Courtemanche. The arson finding came later. And today, there is no public memorial connecting the Grey Nuns fire to the fire station that responded to it. The only marker inside Caserne 10 is a plaque honouring firefighters who died in the line of duty — it lists both addresses, 1625 Sainte-Catherine and 1445 Saint-Mathieu, confirming the continuity between the two buildings. But the worst event in the station's service area — the deaths of sixty infants one block away — is not mentioned.

---

## PART 8: THE NEW STATION
**[~1.5 minutes]**
**[VISUAL: Caserne 10 facade photo — the 1931 Art Deco building]**

Thirteen years after the Grey Nuns fire, the original Caserne 10 was demolished and replaced. The new building went up in 1931, half a block away, at 1445 rue Saint-Mathieu. It was designed by the architects Harold Shorey and S. Douglass Ritchie, and it won the Royal Architectural Institute of Canada award that year.

**[VISUAL: Interior photos, architectural details]**

It's an Art Deco building — part of what architectural historians sometimes call "Style Camillien Houde," after the mayor who commissioned roughly twenty public buildings during the Depression as employment-generating public works. Caserne 10 was built alongside Casernes 23, 31, and 48 — all in the same year, all in that distinctive Montreal Art Deco civic style.

**[VISUAL: Historical apparatus photos — 1926 fire engine, hose wagon, aerial ladder]**

The new building was purpose-built for motorized fire apparatus. No more horse stalls, no more hay lofts. Wide vehicle bays, reinforced floors, modern ventilation. It was a building designed for the twentieth century.

**[VISUAL: Caserne 10 facade, present day — still in active service]**

And it's still in service. Ninety-four years later, firefighters still respond to calls from this building. Caserne 10 is one of the oldest continuously operating fire stations in Montreal — a hundred and fifty years of unbroken service at essentially the same location, through two buildings, two centuries, and one catastrophe that nobody talks about anymore.

---

## PART 9: WHAT A STREET NAME REMEMBERS
**[~1.5 minutes]**
**[VISUAL: Return to the photo of Rue de la Police street sign]**

So what does a street name remember?

Rue de la Police remembers a building. A combined police and fire station that stood on Sainte-Catherine Street from 1875 to 1931. The building is gone. The architect who designed it — Perrault — is a footnote. The lane that served it is still there, still carrying the name that was assigned in 1876, the year after the station opened.

**[VISUAL: Split screen — Rue de la Police today / 1890 Goad Atlas showing the station]**

But follow that name — trace it through the toponymy database, through the fire insurance atlases, through the architectural records, through the newspapers archived at BAnQ — and you find something the street name doesn't tell you. You find the firefighters who lived in that building. The district chief, Marin, who carried children out of a burning dormitory four at a time. The sixty infants who died one block away on a Valentine's Day evening in the last year of the First World War. And a woman named Berthe Courtemanche, whose name didn't appear in any newspaper until months after the ash had cooled.

[PAUSE]

**[VISUAL: Memorial plaque inside Caserne 10]**

The street name is a fossil. But fossils, if you know how to read them, contain entire worlds.

[LONG PAUSE]

**[VISUAL: Fade to title card — "Caserne 10: A Fire Station and Its Neighbourhood, 1875–1931"]**

## PRODUCTION NOTES

**Tone:** Measured, reflective, storytelling. Not academic monotone — this is a narrative with a dramatic arc. Start quiet and curious (the street name), build through discovery (the database, the maps, the station), hit the emotional peak (the fire, the babies, the arson), and resolve with reflection (what a street name remembers).

**Pacing:** Slower in Parts 5 and 7 (the fire and the arson). Let the silences do work. The pauses after the death toll and after the arson reveal are important — don't rush past them.

**Music (optional):** If using background music, keep it minimal and ambient. Something sparse and piano-based would work. Drop it entirely during Part 5 (the fire) and Part 7 (the arson) — let the voice carry those sections alone.

**Visuals sync:** The script is written so that visual transitions align with narrative beats. Each [VISUAL] note indicates when a new image or document should appear. In Miro, these would correspond to zooming into different frames of the board as the narration plays.

**Recording tip:** Record in segments matching the Parts. This makes editing easier and lets you re-record individual sections without redoing the whole thing.
